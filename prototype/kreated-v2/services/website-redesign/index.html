What it is

Before any of the detail.

A redesign improves or replaces a website that already exists, keeping what is already working for you and rebuilding what is not.

The starting point is the site you have, not a blank page. Some redesigns keep the structure and change how it looks and reads. Some keep the brand and rebuild everything underneath it. Which one you need is a finding, not a package — and it is worked out before anything is quoted.

Like every build here, the person who scopes the project is the person who designs and builds it. Who that is, and why it works that way.

When a redesign is the right call

And when it is not.

The site no longer matches the business. Something that was fine to launch with and now argues against the quality of the work it is supposed to represent. This is the most common reason, and the most legitimate.

It cannot carry what you need to add. Service pages, a location page, a proper contact path, or the structure that search work depends on. If the site cannot hold those, adding them is the more expensive route.

It is not findable and the reasons are structural. Sometimes visibility is a content and profile problem on a sound site, in which case local SEO is the cheaper answer and you should be told that. Sometimes the structure itself is the obstacle. Which one comes first is worth reading before you spend anything.

Not because it looks dated to you. A site that is working is allowed to be plain. If nothing is broken and nothing is missing, a redesign is a discretionary purchase and will be described as one.

What carries over

Recognition is an asset, not a starting point to discard.

A redesign that throws away everything spends the recognition you have already paid for. Where the mark, the name and the way people already identify you are working, they carry over. Where the brand itself is the problem, that is a separate piece of work with its own scope — see brand strategy and identity, which can run alongside a build or entirely on its own.

Content usually carries over too, edited rather than rewritten. Where the words genuinely have to be made rather than migrated, that is scoped and priced as its own item rather than absorbed silently.

How the engagement runs

The same shape as any build here.

Scope first, in writing: what the site has to do, which pages exist, what each one is for, and what it costs. Structure decided late is the expensive kind, so it is settled before design starts. The five steps, and where you sign off.

Websites typically take two to four weeks depending on scope, feedback and how quickly content and assets arrive. Payment is 50% to start and 50% before launch. After the final payment the finished site and the design and content created for the project are yours, and you can keep working on it with or without Kreated.

Every site launches search ready and structured so its information can be read correctly by people and by the systems that summarize businesses. That is a build standard, not an add-on.

Questions

Will I lose the pages I already rank for?

Pages worth keeping keep their addresses. Where a page is merged or replaced, a redirect points the old address at the new one so existing links still resolve. What is deliberately not promised is a search outcome. No before-and-after ranking or traffic result is documented for any project here, so this page does not claim one.

Can you keep the copy we already have?

Where it earns its place, yes. And that is usually more of it than owners expect. Existing wording gets tidied and reorganised rather than replaced for the sake of it. What tends to change is the order, the headings and the calls to action.

Do we have to rebuild everything at once?

Not necessarily. If the structure is sound and only the surface has aged, the work is smaller and cheaper than a full rebuild. That is the first thing worth establishing, and it is what the Free Website Audit is for.

What if our site is on a builder like Wix or Squarespace?

That is a normal starting point. What matters is what the site has to do next, not what it was made with. The platform question is settled in scoping, in writing, before anything is rebuilt.

Is a redesign cheaper than a new site?

Often, but not always. It depends on how much survives. A redesign has no package of its own: it is scoped and priced against the same Launch and Growth engagements, because the work is the same work whether the starting point is a tired site or a blank page.

Can I see a redesign you have actually done?

Yes. Rare Raleigh Restoration is on this page above, the real previous site and the real site that is live now, both captured, with no metric attached to either.

How long does a redesign take?

Most website projects take about 2–4 weeks, depending on scope, feedback, and how quickly we receive the content and assets needed to build. A redesign sits in the same range, and often at the shorter end when a good amount of the existing content survives.

How does payment work, and who owns the result?

Standard website projects are 50% to start and 50% before launch; larger or custom projects may use milestones defined in the proposal. After final payment you own the finished website and the design and content created specifically for you. The full answer is on the Web Design page.

Will my rankings drop right after launch?

A short unsettled period is normal while search engines recrawl and reassess the site. What causes lasting damage is not the redesign itself but the avoidable mistakes around it: addresses changed without redirects, pages that were already performing rewritten, internal links broken, crawling blocked at launch. Those are planned for rather than discovered afterward, and the site is watched after launch rather than handed over and forgotten. Nobody can promise positions will be identical, and this page does not.

What happens to my old page addresses?

Every existing address is listed before anything changes and mapped to exactly one address on the new site. Redirects are set at the server rather than in the browser, and chains where one redirect points at another are avoided, because each additional hop costs something.
