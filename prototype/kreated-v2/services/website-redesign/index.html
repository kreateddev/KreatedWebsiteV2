What it is

And how it differs from a rebuild.

A website redesign improves or replaces an existing site while keeping what already works: the pages that earn attention, the content that is already correct, and the addresses those pages already live at.

A cosmetic refresh changes how a site looks. A structural redesign also changes how it is organized, how its pages relate to each other and how clearly it explains the business. A rebuild starts again from nothing. Most businesses need the middle option, because the problem is usually structure and clarity rather than the visual style alone.

Redesigns do not cost rankings. Badly handled migrations do. The damage comes from a specific set of avoidable mistakes: page addresses that change with no redirect pointing the old one at the new, content rewritten on the pages that were already earning attention, internal links left broken, and crawling accidentally blocked at launch.

The way that is avoided is unglamorous. Every existing address is inventoried before anything changes, each one is mapped to exactly one new address, redirects are set at the server rather than in the browser, chains of redirects are avoided, and the pages that already perform keep their content rather than being rewritten for the sake of it.

Expect a short unsettled period after launch while search engines recrawl and reassess the site. That is normal and it is monitored rather than ignored. Nobody can promise that positions will be identical afterward, and this page will not.

When a redesign beats patching

Patching is cheaper right up until it isn’t.

Patch it when the problem is local: a page that reads badly, a form nobody can find, a section that has gone out of date. Those are edits, and a redesign is an expensive way to make one.

Redesign it when the problem is structural, when the site is organized around what the business used to do, when the layout fights every new page you add, when it was never really built for a phone, or when the quality of the work and the quality of the site have visibly drifted apart. At that point each patch costs more than it returns.

If there is no site at all, this is the wrong service. That is Web Design & Development.

What gets looked at first

Before anything is redrawn.

  • What the site is actually organized around Which pages exist, what each one is for, and which ones exist only because they always have.
  • What a first-time visitor sees in the first seconds Whether the page says what the business does, for whom, and where, before anyone scrolls.
  • The path to contacting you How many steps, how obvious, and whether it survives on a phone with one thumb.
  • What is worth keeping Copy, pages and URLs that already earn their place. A redesign is not an excuse to throw away work that still works.
  • How it behaves at real screen sizes Not a shrunk desktop layout. A rebuilt one.

Moving without breaking things

The part people find out about afterward.

Where a page still deserves to exist, its address is kept. Where it is merged or replaced, a redirect points the old address at the new one, so links other people have already published keep working.

Questions

Will I lose the pages I already rank for?

Pages worth keeping keep their addresses. Where a page is merged or replaced, a redirect points the old address at the new one so existing links still resolve.

What is deliberately not promised is a search outcome. No before-and-after ranking or traffic result is documented for any project here, so this page does not claim one.

Can you keep the copy we already have?

Where it earns its place, yes. And that is usually more of it than owners expect. Existing wording gets tidied and reorganised rather than replaced for the sake of it. What tends to change is the order, the headings and the calls to action.

Do we have to rebuild everything at once?

Not necessarily. If the structure is sound and only the surface has aged, the work is smaller and cheaper than a full rebuild. That is the first thing worth establishing, and it is what the Free Website Audit is for.

What if our site is on a builder like Wix or Squarespace?

That is a normal starting point. What matters is what the site has to do next, not what it was made with. The platform question is settled in scoping, in writing, before anything is rebuilt.

Is a redesign cheaper than a new site?

Often, but not always. It depends on how much survives. A redesign has no package of its own: it is scoped and priced against the same Launch and Growth engagements, because the work is the same work whether the starting point is a tired site or a blank page.

Can I see a redesign you have actually done?

Yes. Rare Raleigh Restoration is on this page above, the real previous site and the real site that is live now, both captured, with no metric attached to either.

How long does a redesign take?

Most website projects take about 2–4 weeks, depending on scope, feedback, and how quickly we receive the content and assets needed to build. A redesign sits in the same range, and often at the shorter end when a good amount of the existing content survives.

How does payment work, and who owns the result?

Standard website projects are 50% to start and 50% before launch; larger or custom projects may use milestones defined in the proposal.

After final payment you own the finished website and the design and content created specifically for you. The full answer is on the Web Design page.

Will my rankings drop right after launch?

A short unsettled period is normal while search engines recrawl and reassess the site. What causes lasting damage is not the redesign itself but the avoidable mistakes around it: addresses changed without redirects, pages that were already performing rewritten, internal links broken, crawling blocked at launch.

Those are planned for rather than discovered afterward, and the site is watched after launch rather than handed over and forgotten. Nobody can promise positions will be identical, and this page does not.

What happens to my old page addresses?

Every existing address is listed before anything changes and mapped to exactly one address on the new site. Redirects are set at the server rather than in the browser, and chains where one redirect points at another are avoided, because each additional hop costs something.
