A remodel is the largest thing most homeowners ever buy from a contractor. They take weeks to choose, compare more than one bid, and decide mostly on finished work they can see. The website is where that comparison happens.

How a remodeling client decides

Planned, compared, and read more than once.

A kitchen, an addition or a whole-home remodel is not an emergency. It is planned, researched and discussed, often by two people, and the website is read several times before anyone calls.

That changes what the site is for. An urgent trade needs to be fast. A remodeler needs to be convincing on the third visit, when someone is showing the site to a spouse and asking whether this is the contractor they trust with the house.

What they are looking for is evidence: projects like theirs, finished; a sense of how the work runs once it starts; proof that you are licensed and insured; and some idea of what it costs. A site that answers those questions does part of the selling before the first walk-through.

Where remodeling sites lose the project

The usual reasons a good contractor is passed over.

  • A gallery with no story Forty photos in one grid, no captions and no project types. Someone planning a kitchen cannot find the kitchens, and cannot tell what was actually done in any of them.
  • Every service on one page Additions, kitchens, baths, basements and decks as a list. The person planning an addition wants a page about additions: what is involved, what it needs, and the ones you have built.
  • No sense of the process The hardest part of a remodel to buy is the unknown: how long, who is in the house, and what happens when something is found behind a wall. A site that explains how you work answers the question a bid cannot.
  • Credentials implied, not stated North Carolina licenses general contractors and the register is public. When the next contractor prints a license number and you do not, the careful buyer notices.
  • No clear first step A “contact us” form with nine fields. The real first step for most remodels is a conversation or a walk-through, and the site should say what that is and how to book it.

None of these is fixed by a new color scheme, which is why a redesign that only changes the look rarely changes the enquiries.

What gets built

Organized around how the project is bought.

  • Project pages by type. Additions, kitchens, bathrooms, whole-home: whichever you want more of, each with the projects that belong on it and what was done, from your own photos.
  • A page for each kind of work. A service page can be found, compared and sent to a spouse; a line in a list cannot. How many service pages a website needs covers how many.
  • Your process, written down. The way you actually run a job, from the first visit to the final walk-through, in your words. Nothing is promised on the page that the contract does not.
  • License, insurance and warranty. Stated plainly where you hold them. Nothing is claimed on your behalf that you have not confirmed.
  • A first step people can take. A short consultation request that works on a phone, and a number that is real text.
  • A Google profile that agrees with the site. Same services, same area, same details. That is Google Business Profile work, and it matters as much as the site.

Questions

Should a remodeling website show prices?

Usually not a fixed price, because no two remodels are the same. A starting range, or the factors that move the number, filters out the wrong enquiries early and saves a walk-through nobody needed. What the site should never do is imply a price the contract will not honor.

Do I need a separate page for each type of project?

For each type you want more of, yes. Someone planning an addition searches for additions, and a page that answers that one project, with the ones you have built, does more than a line in a list of everything you do.

Can the site use my own project photos?

Yes, and it should. Finished work is what a remodeling client decides on. If the photos are thin, that is said plainly at the start rather than covered with stock photography, which proves nothing about your work.

How long does a remodeling contractor website take?

Most take about two to four weeks, depending on scope, how quickly feedback comes back, and how quickly the photos and content arrive. The scope and the price are agreed in writing before anything starts.
