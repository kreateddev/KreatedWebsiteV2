RESOURCES / WHEN TO REBRAND

When a rebrand is worth doing,and when it is an expensive distraction.

The useful question is not whether the identity looks dated. It is whether it still describes the business you actually run now.

The short answer

One test.

Rebrand when the business has changed and the identity has not. Everything else is either tidying, which is cheaper, or a sales problem wearing a design costume, which a rebrand will not fix.

Most businesses asking this question do not need a new identity. They need the one they have to be applied consistently, which is a much smaller job and produces most of the same benefit.

When it is genuinely warranted

The business moved and the identity stayed.

  • You sell something different now A handyman operation that became a remodeling company. A repair business that now does full installs. When the name or the mark describes the older, smaller version, it argues against the work you currently want.
  • The customer changed Moving from homeowners to commercial, or from small jobs to larger ones, changes who has to take you seriously in the first few seconds. An identity built for the previous buyer works against you.
  • The name limits you A town in the name after you expanded past it. A trade in the name after you added three more. This is the one case where a name change, not just a visual one, may be on the table, and it is a decision with real costs attached.
  • The identity is visibly below the standard of the work The jobs are excellent and the presentation says otherwise. This is the most common legitimate reason and the one owners feel long before they act on it.
  • Nothing agrees with anything The logo on the truck, the one on the invoice and the one on the site are not quite the same drawing, and nobody can say which is correct. Sometimes that is a consistency job. When there is no agreed original left to return to, it is a rebrand.

When not to

Said by someone who sells the work.

  • You are bored of it. You see your own logo hundreds of times a week. Your customers see it occasionally, and familiarity is worth something you would be spending to discard.
  • A style has moved on. Chasing a look dates a business faster than an older mark that has been used consistently for a decade.
  • The phone is quiet. This is the expensive mistake. A quiet phone is usually visibility or conversion, not identity, and a rebrand leaves both untouched at considerable cost. Establish which end is broken first: website or search work.
  • A competitor rebranded. Not a reason.
  • The business is about to change again. If the shape is still moving, wait. Rebranding twice in three years costs more than the delay and confuses the customers who did notice.

The cheaper thing to try first

It resolves this more often than a rebrand does.

Before commissioning a new identity, apply the existing one properly for a month. One version of the mark everywhere. One typeface. The same colors on the site, the van, the invoice and the proposal. The same sentence describing the business in all of them.

A surprising number of businesses that felt they needed a rebrand needed this instead, and it costs an afternoon of decisions. If the result still looks below the standard of the work, that is real evidence rather than a feeling, and the rebrand is now a decision rather than a hope.
