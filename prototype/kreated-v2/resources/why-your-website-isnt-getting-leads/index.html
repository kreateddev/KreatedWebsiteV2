RESOURCES / NO LEADS FROM YOUR WEBSITE

Your website gets visitorsand no calls. Here is how to find out why.

It is almost always one of six things, and every one of them is checkable rather than a matter of opinion.

The short answer

Before the detail.

A website that gets visitors and produces no calls is failing at one of six things: what it says, how someone contacts you, how it behaves on a phone, whether a stranger trusts it, whether the right people find it at all, or something technical getting in the way.

They are not equally likely, and they are not equally expensive to fix. The mistake that costs owners the most money is assuming it is the first thing they can see, which is usually the design.

The six, and how each one fails

In roughly the order they cost work.

  • 1. Clarity, what the business actually does A visitor decides in a few seconds whether they are in the right place. A landscaper whose homepage says “transforming outdoor spaces” and never says the words retaining wall, drainage or sod is asking the visitor to work it out. Most will not. This is the most common failure and the cheapest to fix, because it is words.
  • 2. The path to contacting you Count the taps between landing and reaching you. If the number is in an image, if the form asks for a budget range before a name, if the only option is email when the job is urgent, the visit ends. A plumbing emergency at 9pm does not fill in a contact form.
  • 3. How it behaves on a phone Most local service traffic is mobile. Text that needs pinching, a menu that will not close, a button under a sticky bar. This one is easy to miss because owners check their own site on a laptop.
  • 4. Whether a stranger trusts you Referrals arrive already trusting you. Search traffic does not. No photographs of your own work, no reviews, no named person, no license or insurance detail where the trade expects it, and a careful buyer keeps looking.
  • 5. Whether the right people find it The site may be fine and simply invisible for the searches that matter. One page covering nine services, no page naming the towns you cover, or a Google listing that disagrees with the site. This is the failure that looks like a website problem and is not.
  • 6. Technical friction Slow loading, pages that cannot be read properly by search engines, a contact form that has been quietly failing for months. Rarer than owners fear, but genuinely fatal when present.

Why owners usually guess wrong

The visible problem is not usually the expensive one.

Design is the only one of the six you can see without checking anything. So when the phone is quiet, the site looking dated is the explanation that presents itself first, and a rebuild is the purchase that follows.

Sometimes that is right. A site that argues against the quality of the work is a real problem, and Website Redesign exists for it. But a rebuild fixes clarity, contact path, mobile and trust. It does not, by itself, fix being invisible. If the actual failure is number five, a beautiful new site produces the same silence at greater expense, which is the single most common way local businesses waste money on a website.

The distinction is worth being sure about before spending, and it is not something you can settle by looking at your own site. You already know what it means.

If it turns out to be visibility

Two different fixes, often confused.

Being hard to find splits into two pieces of work. Google Business Profile is the listing itself: categories, services, area, hours, the information Google shows before anyone reaches your site. Local SEO is the wider system around it, including the pages on your own site that have to exist before they can rank.

Which comes first depends on what you already have. Local SEO and your Google Business Profile are not the same thing works through that.
