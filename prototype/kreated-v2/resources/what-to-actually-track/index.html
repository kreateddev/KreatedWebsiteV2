RESOURCES / WHAT TO ACTUALLY TRACK

What a small business shouldactually track. It is four things.

Most reporting measures activity because activity is easy to count. The four numbers below are about money and they are the ones worth an owner’s attention.

The four

In the order they matter.

  • 1. Inquiries per month Calls, forms and messages from people who want to buy something. One number, counted the same way every month. Not visits, not impressions. If this is flat while everything else rises, nothing that matters has changed.
  • 2. How many became customers Of those inquiries, how many turned into paid work. This is the number that decides whether the answer is more marketing or better follow-up, and it is the one almost nobody writes down. Fifteen inquiries and two jobs is a quoting or response problem, and no amount of additional traffic fixes it.
  • 3. What the work was worth Average job value, roughly. Twelve inquiries a month is a different business at four hundred dollars a job than at nine thousand, and the difference decides what marketing is worth doing.
  • 4. Where they came from, approximately Roughly: found on Google, referral, repeat customer, saw the van. “Approximately” is doing real work in that sentence — see below. Even a rough split tells you which channel is carrying the business and which one you are paying for out of habit.

What cannot be measured cleanly

Anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.

A customer hears about you from a neighbor. Three weeks later they search your name, look at two other businesses, see your van in the neighborhood, and call the number on it. No analytics tool records that journey, and any report that assigns it neatly to one channel is assigning it arbitrarily.

Phone calls are the specific hole. Most inquiries to a local service business arrive by phone, and a website analytics tool cannot see a phone call at all. This is why the single most valuable measurement tool for a local business is a person asking “how did you hear about us?” and writing the answer down.

So treat attribution as a rough split, not an accounting. Directionally right and honest beats precise and invented.

What is not worth your attention

Real numbers, wrong audience.

  • Traffic on its own. Sessions rise for reasons that have nothing to do with buyers. A month with more visits and fewer inquiries is a worse month.
  • Bounce rate. Someone reads the page, gets the phone number and calls. That records as a bounce, and it was the best outcome available.
  • Keyword rankings as a headline. Position varies by who is searching and where they are standing. Useful to a practitioner diagnosing a page, misleading as a monthly score.
  • Impressions. Worth watching in the first months of a new site, because impressions arrive before clicks do. Not a business result.
  • Social followers. Unless you can name a job that came from there.
  • Anything in a report you do not act on. If a number has never once changed a decision, it is costing attention and returning nothing.

How to track it without a system

A spreadsheet is genuinely enough.

One row per inquiry: date, how they found you, what they wanted, whether it became a job, and what it was worth. Filled in as they come, it takes a few seconds each and it answers every question above.

The tools help underneath this. Analytics shows which pages people arrive on, and a Google Business Profile reports calls and direction requests from the listing itself. Both are useful context. Neither replaces the row, because neither one knows what happened after the phone rang.

Two months of that, and questions that used to be arguments become readable: whether the search work is producing anything (and whether enough time has passed to expect it to), whether the site is turning interest into inquiries (or not), and whether the spending is justified at all (sometimes it is not).
