RESOURCES / CONTRACTOR WEBSITE CHECKLIST

The contractor website checklist:what to look for on the site you have.

Eleven things worth checking on your own site before anyone quotes you for a new one. Most take a phone and five minutes.

How to use this

On a phone, as a stranger.

Open your own site on your phone, on cellular data, and go through the list below as if you had never seen the business before. Most contractor sites fail three or four of these, and the failures are usually cheap to fix once you can name them.

Check it as a stranger, not as the owner. You know what you do and where you work; the visitor deciding between you and two other numbers does not.

What the visitor needs immediately

The first fifteen seconds.

  • 1. It says what you actually do, in words people search Water heater replacement, drain cleaning, water damage restoration. Not “quality workmanship since 2009”. If a stranger cannot name your trade from the top of the homepage, nothing below matters.
  • 2. It says where you work, with real place names A phrase like “and surrounding areas” leaves a homeowner in Clayton to work out for themselves whether that includes them. A competitor who wrote the word Clayton has already answered it.
  • 3. Calling takes one tap The number is real text, not part of an image, and it dials when tapped. Test it. A number that does not dial is one of the most common and most expensive faults on trades sites.
  • 4. The form is short enough to finish one-handed Name, number, what happened. A form asking for budget range and project timeline before a name is a form for someone who is not in a hurry, and your buyer usually is.

What makes a stranger believe you

Referrals arrive trusting you. Search traffic does not.

  • 5. Photographs of your own finished work Not stock photography. Restoration, remodeling and exterior trades are judged on before and after, and a page of purchased images reads says nothing about the standard of your work, because it is not your work.
  • 6. A real business identity The legal name, a real address or a stated service-area policy, and a way to tell a person is behind it. Anonymous sites lose to named ones.
  • 7. Licensing and insurance where you hold them Stated plainly, in the trades where a customer expects it. Never claimed where it is not held.
  • 8. Consistent presentation One logo, one set of colors, one way the business describes itself. A site assembled from three eras of the business reads as neglected, which costs credibility a good business has earned.

What decides whether anyone arrives

The part that is invisible from the homepage.

  • 9. A page for each service you want more of Not everything gathered onto a single Services page. A page of its own is something you can send to a customer asking about that one job, and something a search can land on; a line in a list is neither. How many service pages your website actually needs works out the number.
  • 10. The site and the Google listing agree Same services, same area, same hours, same number. Check both side by side; disagreements are common and cost nothing but attention to fix.
  • 11. You can tell whether any of it is working Something recording calls and form submissions, so a decision about next year is made on evidence. What to actually track covers the short version.

Items 9 to 11 are the ones owners skip, because unlike a broken phone link they are invisible until you go looking.

If you would rather have it checked
